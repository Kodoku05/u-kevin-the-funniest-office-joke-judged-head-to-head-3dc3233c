# Candidate review

Read the published judge brief in the kit's RULES.txt before drafting. These are editorial assessments, not results from the house judge. Scores run from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong) and are compared in criterion order, never added into a total.

## Criteria, in published priority order

1. A real one-line joke about office life, with a setup and a turn.
2. A situation recognizable to someone who has worked in an office.
3. A surprising turn rather than a complaint.
4. Economical wording, one line, punchline last.
5. Freshness rather than a familiar circulating office joke.

The board is blind, as specified in the task; no live competitive rank or unrecorded judge result is claimed. The playbook's useful structural test is a high-status victim, a perverse fix with a concrete consequence, and a deadpan close. Avoid the crowded printer, plant, meeting-about-meetings, and HR survey premises.

**A:** I asked IT to fix the Wi-Fi, so they moved my desk next to the router and closed the ticket: 'user relocated, issue resolved.'

Real setup and bureaucratic reversal; Wi-Fi and IT are universal. Relocating a desk is a plausible workaround rather than a big surprise. The closing explanation repeats the action, and there is no high-status victim.

**B:** The CEO banned meetings on Fridays, so we now hold a Monday meeting to plan how to spend our meeting-free Friday.

Clear office joke, but the reversal is expected and wordy. Reject the crowded meeting-about-meetings premise.

**C:** I replied-all to say 'please stop replying-all'; HR replied-all to confirm my request had been noted by all.

Very recognizable and neatly circular, but the second reply-all is visible from the setup. It escalates repetition rather than producing a new consequence.

**D:** I told my boss the printer was out of toner, so he printed a 40-page report on how to save toner.

Recognizable waste with a clear contradiction, but printing while out of toner also strains the setup. The familiar printer premise is specifically excluded by the strategy and is not stronger than the alternatives.

**E:** The fire alarm went off mid-presentation, so the CEO finished the deck from the car park and marked himself 'working remotely'.

Good concrete image and a strong closing reinterpretation. Office evacuation during a CEO presentation is less everyday than reply-all or phishing; the CEO continues the problem instead of someone perversely solving it.

**F:** HR sent a survey asking if we're happy at work; the only honest answer got flagged as a phishing attempt.

A joke with a turn, but the content of the honest answer is missing, so the phishing classification needs an extra inference. Reject the crowded HR survey premise.

## Three original candidates

**G:** The CEO demanded everyone stop wasting time on reply-all, so IT disabled his Send button. Ticket closed: root cause removed.

Strong runner-up: recognizable reply-all irritation, a powerful victim, a consequential fix, and a compact ticket closure. Its weakness is that blaming the CEO becomes fairly predictable once IT starts acting.

**H, first version:** The CEO kept clicking phishing links, so IT replaced his mouse with a stapler. Ticket closed: all attachments now secure.

Provisional winner. A familiar security problem becomes a physical office scene: the CEO gets a stapler instead of a mouse. The closing ticket status then reinterprets attachments as stapled paperwork. The pun supports an actual absurd action rather than substituting for one. Risk: the reader must connect email attachments with stationery; the physical stapler makes that connection concrete. The close has two removable words.

**I:** The CEO demanded we roll back every bad decision, so IT restored the previous CEO. Ticket closed: last known good configuration.

A clear leadership reversal with a deadpan technical close, but restoring a previous CEO requires a bigger leap than replacing a mouse. Backup terminology is also less universal than phishing warnings and staplers.

## Selection before first run

Choose H over runner-up G. Both are strong on criterion 1 (a real office joke) and criterion 2 (widely recognizable office behavior). The first difference is criterion 3: H's mouse-to-stapler substitution and physical meaning of attachments are less predictable than discovering that the CEO caused the reply-all problem. G is slightly tighter on criterion 4, but that does not outweigh H's stronger surprise. No claim of a measured competitive margin is made.

After the first successful run, re-read the brief and revise the closing phrase in a separate write, then verify the revised entry again.

## Second pass after the first successful run

Re-read the published judge brief, especially criterion 4 and "Length for its own sake does not count." Replace "all attachments now secure" with "attachments secured". This removes seven characters, retains both meanings of attachments, and makes the final phrase sound more like a terse IT resolution. The final word also completes the physical stapling action. Criterion 4 improves from 4 to 5; the other scores stay unchanged.

**H, final (114 characters / 114 bytes, counted with Node):** The CEO kept clicking phishing links, so IT replaced his mouse with a stapler. Ticket closed: attachments secured.

Final ordered scores: **5, 5, 4, 5, 4**. H still beats G first at criterion 3; the revision preserves that surprise while removing the runner-up's wording advantage.
